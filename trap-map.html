01

Placement Doctrine

Why these sites.
PARA 2A — INTELLIGENCE BASIS

With the fog machine down, we're fighting larvae — not adults. Every trap site on this map was chosen by layering four factors:

Water

Proximity to known or likely larval habitat — ditches, low spots, the lagoon, stock water, and persistent standing water after rain.

Wind

Summer prevailing winds in eastern SD blow S to SSE. Traps placed downwind of water sources intercept gravid females heading toward homes.

Cover

Culex tarsalis seeks shaded, sheltered water — full-sun containers see far less egg-laying than the same container in partial shade. Tree lines, church grounds, fencerows, and outbuildings score highest and now drive site selection directly, not just as a tiebreaker: six of the sites below (T14–T19) exist specifically because they sit under mature tree cover.

Buffer

Traps must sit at property edges — away from where people sit, sleep, or play — but between water sources and gathering areas. The school, park, ballfield, and residential blocks define the buffer.

Cx. tarsalis disperses 1–3 km from larval sites on average, up to 10+ miles in open country. In a town this size (0.59 sq mi), every water source is within dispersal range of every home. That means distributed trapping — many small traps across town — beats a few big ones in one spot.

The original 13 sites leaned heavily on the perimeter (Layer 2 below). Shaded interior ground — church tree lines and the leafier residential blocks — was under-covered, so this pass adds six interior/shade sites (T14–T19) to fill it in. See Layer 3.

Each site is scored on four factors. Higher total = higher priority.

South edge — lagoon drainage intercept

Why here: Directly north of the wastewater lagoon along the drainage path. Prevailing S/SSE wind pushes gravid females from the lagoon straight through this corridor toward the residential blocks. Intercepting them here keeps them away from the densest housing.

Trap type: Storage tote or half-barrel with Bti dunk. Shelter the trap against fenceline or outbuilding for shade.

Notes: Highest-value single site. The lagoon is the largest persistent water body within dispersal range and a known Cx. tarsalis producer in small-town SD.

Trap type: Bucket trap — small footprint, easy to secure. Coordinate with the school. Bti is nontoxic but the trap should be out of reach.

The Three Layers

Defense in depth.
PARA 2D — LAYERED DEFENSE MODEL

Effective coverage works in concentric layers — not random scatter. Deploy from the outside in.

Layer 1

Source reduction. Drain, dump, or Bti-treat every body of standing water you can reach. The lagoon, ditches, low spots, old tires, gutters. Every egg raft killed at the source is 200–300 mosquitoes that never fly. This layer matters most.

Layer 2

Perimeter intercept. Traps at property and town edges — the T1–T4 ring — catch gravid females heading inbound from water sources to lay eggs. Place them downwind of water, upwind of people.

Layer 3

Interior fill. Traps in backyards and between buildings catch anything that got past the perimeter. These address the container habitat that exists inside town — the backyard bucket, the old tire, the tarp fold. T8 and the six shaded interior sites (T14–T19) make up this layer.

You don't need all 19 sites to make a difference. The Priority 1 sites alone cover the highest-risk corridors. Add P2 and P3 as you have traps and hands — the shaded interior sites (T14–T19) are the best P2/P3 return for a yard that never gets direct sun.

05

Wind & Timing

Use the weather.
PARA 2E — ENVIRONMENTAL FACTORS
WIND INTELLIGENCE — EASTERN SD SUMMER

Why wind matters for trap placement

Eastern South Dakota's prevailing summer winds blow from the south to south-southeast (S/SSE), typically 8–15 mph. Cx. tarsalis is a strong flier (up to 10+ miles), but even strong fliers follow the wind when dispersing from larval sites to feeding areas.

That means adults emerging from the lagoon and south drainage ditch are pushed north into town on the prevailing wind. The Priority 1 trap line (T1–T4) is oriented perpendicular to this flow — a net across the path.

On days when the wind shifts east or west, the P2 sites on the east (T7) and west (T5) edges catch the crosswind traffic. The system works because it covers the dominant direction and the alternates.

Peak risk hours: Cx. tarsalis is most active at dusk and after dark. Wind typically drops at dusk, reducing dispersal distance — which means more adults stay closer to their larval source. Traps close to water sources (T1, T6) are most effective during these calm-wind, high-activity hours.

Season context: we are mid-August — at peak WNV risk. See the season chart. Every trap placed now is working during the highest-risk weeks of the year.

Deploy Checklist

Before you emplace.
PARA 2F — DEPLOYMENT PROCEDURE
PRE-DEPLOYMENT CHECKLIST
  1. Read the Do Not Emplace list. Seriously.
  2. Build your trap from Build Plans. Bucket is fine. Kiddie pool if you've got room.
  3. Buy Bti — Mosquito Dunks or Bits. See Resupply. About $10.
  4. Walk this map. Find your nearest site. Check it on the ground: is there shade? Is it accessible for weekly refills? Is it away from where kids play?
  5. Place the trap at a property edge — near a fence, shed, or tree line. Never next to a door, deck, or playground.
  6. Add the Bti per label directions. Top up weekly. Replace dunks monthly.
  7. Tell your neighbors. A trap network works when it's a network. The more of the 19 sites covered, the better the town-wide suppression.
